The 5-Strategy Stack
Getting It to Actually Stick
Five evidence-backed strategies translated into real classroom moves — written by a CCMA teacher, for CCMA teachers.
📋 NHA CCMA Aligned 🏫 High School Seniors ✅ Any Classroom Structure ⚡ Low Prep
Here's the honest truth: most retention problems in a CCMA classroom aren't a student motivation problem — they're a study method problem. Seniors have been trained to re-read their notes the night before a test. For history class, that might work. For NHA content, it almost never does. The five strategies below are different. They're backed by decades of learning science, and more importantly, they're practical enough to actually use in whatever classroom structure you already have. You don't need to overhaul your entire course. You need to shift a few habits — yours and theirs.
1
Strategy One
Retrieval Practice — Quizzing Beats Reviewing, Every Time
🧠

If your students are staring at their notes before the NHA practice test, they feel like they're studying. They're not. Re-reading creates familiarity — the illusion of knowing. Retrieval practice forces the brain to actually reconstruct information from scratch, which is exactly what the exam requires.

The research on this is overwhelming, but the classroom version is simple: make students recall information before you confirm it, not after. The struggle to pull something from memory — even when they get it wrong — does more for long-term retention than reading the correct answer twice ever will.

📌 What This Looks Like in CCMA

Start class with a no-notes, no-pressure recall question — out loud, not written. "Without looking — what are the five rights of medication administration?" Let students call out answers. Write them on the board. Then confirm. Three minutes, zero prep, and the retention payoff compounds over weeks. Do this every single day and watch what happens to practice test scores by Week 10.

Reality check: Students will resist this at first. They feel exposed when they can't answer. Normalize it early: "We're training your brain to retrieve, not just recognize. The NHA exam doesn't show you the answer first — so we're not going to either." Frame the struggle as the point, not a sign of failure.
Try It →

Pick 3 high-frequency NHA topics from your most recent unit. Write one question per index card. Pull one card at the start of class every day this week — popcorn style, no hands raised, just call on someone. That's it. Start tomorrow.

2
Strategy Two
Spaced Repetition — Review Before They Forget, Not After
📅

Here's a painful reality: if you teach infection control in Week 5 and don't touch it again until Week 18 exam prep, your students have forgotten most of it by Week 9. That's not a reflection of their ability — it's just how memory works. Spaced repetition is the practice of bringing material back at increasing intervals, before the forgetting fully sets in.

You don't need a new app or system. You just need to be intentional about reaching back into previous content once or twice a week — even for five minutes.

📌 What This Looks Like in CCMA

Once a week, spend the first 5 minutes of class on a topic from 2–3 weeks ago. One question. One quick scenario. One mnemonic review. You covered vital signs three weeks ago? Bring it back briefly while you're teaching patient intake — show students why it connects. The callbacks don't have to be long. They just have to be consistent.

Try It →

Open your pacing guide right now and block one day per week — even just the first 5 minutes — as a callback slot. Label it "Review: [topic from 2–3 weeks prior]." Fill in the topics now so you don't have to think about it in the moment. That's the whole system.

3
Strategy Three
Clinical Anchoring — Connect Every Concept to a Real Patient Moment
🏥

Seniors disengage from content that feels like trivia. "What is the normal range for adult diastolic blood pressure?" feels like a test question. "Your patient's BP just came back 150/95 — what do you do next?" feels like a job. The second version is also what the NHA exam actually tests.

Clinical anchoring means every concept gets tied to a patient moment before you move on. Not a long detour — just a sentence or two that places the information in a real clinical context students can picture themselves in. If they can see themselves doing it on a Tuesday morning, they'll remember it on exam day.

📌 What This Looks Like in CCMA

After teaching the chain of infection, don't just move to the next slide. Ask: "A patient comes in coughing. Walk me through every point in this room where the chain could break — starting from the waiting area." Students who can narrate a patient scenario will recall the concept far better than students who memorized six links on a chart. The story is what sticks.

Reality check: You don't have to build elaborate scenarios every day. A single anchor sentence is enough: "This is the concept — here's where you'd see it on a real shift." That's it. The more you do it, the more natural it becomes, and students start making those connections themselves.
Try It →

For your next lesson, take the first 3 vocabulary terms you're teaching and write one clinical sentence for each — not a definition, a moment. "This is where you'd see this term on the job." Practice saying it out loud before class. It takes about 10 minutes of prep and it changes how students receive new content.

4
Strategy Four
Low-Stakes Accountability — Small Wins Beat High-Stakes Testing
🏅

High-stakes testing is a terrible retention strategy. When the only grade that matters is the final exam or the NHA certification test, students cram, dump, and forget. Low-stakes accountability — frequent, small, visible moments of demonstrating mastery — changes the rhythm entirely and keeps seniors engaged across a long course.

This doesn't have to be complicated. The goal is a steady drumbeat of small wins that give students evidence of their own growth before the big test arrives. When they sit down for the NHA exam, they should already know they're ready — because they've been proving it to themselves all year.

📌 What This Looks Like in CCMA

End each unit with a short skills check-off — 5 to 8 observable items, no letter grade, just demonstrated or not yet demonstrated. Keep it low pressure but make it visible: a stamp in a skills tracker, a check on a wall chart, a sticker on a printed rubric. Something tangible. Seniors respond to concrete evidence of progress in a way that gradebook numbers alone don't produce.

Try It →

If you don't have check-offs yet, start with just one for your next unit. Write 5 observable skills — things you can watch a student do, not just answer on paper. Create a simple checklist. A dollar store stamp or even a handwritten checkmark makes it feel more official than you'd expect. Try it once and see how differently students engage with check-off day versus test day.

5
Strategy Five
Game-Based Review — 15 Minutes That Actually Move the Needle
🎮

Games work — not just because they're fun, but because they create low-stakes retrieval under mild social pressure, which is surprisingly close to what the NHA exam feels like. The problem is that games can eat your entire class period if you're not disciplined about the format.

The rule: keep review games to 15 minutes max, one concept, high energy in and clean exit out. Use them at the start of class to warm up brains, or at the end of class to consolidate a heavy content day. A well-timed 12-minute game does more for retention than 40 minutes of re-teaching the same slide.

📌 What This Looks Like in CCMA

A digital vocabulary game at the start of class gets students loud, competitive, and recalling terminology without realizing they're studying. A card sort at the end of class — "match the code type to the scenario" — closes a billing lesson with active retrieval instead of passive note-taking. Neither takes more than 15 minutes. Both work better than a review worksheet.

Reality check: Not every game has to be digital. A whiteboard relay, a paper card sort, a "fastest team to write the chain of infection" challenge — these take zero technology and two minutes to set up. Build a small library of 5–10 minute formats and rotate them so the novelty stays fresh.
Try It →

Pick one game format you already have — Kahoot, Quizlet Live, a Canva activity, a card sort, anything — and schedule it into a specific class slot next week. Treat it like an appointment on your lesson plan, not a "if we have time" afterthought. After three weeks of this, it becomes part of your rhythm instead of something you have to remember to do.

These five strategies aren't magic. They're a rhythm.

You don't have to implement all five at once. Start with retrieval practice — one question at the start of class tomorrow. Add spaced repetition the week after. Stack them slowly and the cumulative effect on your students' scores, and your own sanity, will surprise you.
